What the 682% figure actually measured — and what it did not

First, a distinction the whole category blurs: “clinically tested” and “clinically proven” are not the same words. Tested means a study was run. On its own it tells you nothing about what was found, how many people were in it, or whether there was a placebo group.

So here is the precise version of ours, because the precision is the point.

GF-9 has been shown in a double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial to increase mean, serum (blood) growth hormone levels by up to 682%. All other benefits noted in these materials have not been studied in relation to the GF-9 compound.

Read that carefully. It is a measurement of growth hormone in blood. It is not a measurement of muscle gained, fat lost, or years reversed — and we are not going to imply that it is. Any brand converting a blood-marker result directly into a body-composition promise has left the science behind.

The trial was randomized, double-blind and placebo-controlled, run at Pennington Biomedical Research Center — an independent academic institution — and registered publicly as NCT01540773. Sixteen participants, growth hormone measured over a 120-minute window.

Small study, short window, and we are telling you both numbers rather than letting you find them somewhere else. What the figure does tell you is that the mechanism is real and measurable. What it does not tell you is exactly what you personally will feel. That is why the guarantee exists.
